## Confirmed facts (2+ sources each)
- ORIGIN: a stray, brindle-and-white "bull terrier" type mutt of unknown breed (NYT 1926; Military Times: breed "has never been known"). Birth date unknown (Wikipedia: "1915 or earlier"). Do not state a breed or a birthday.
- WHERE FOUND: summer 1917 (July, per Wikipedia), while the 102nd Infantry (Connecticut troops, 26th "Yankee" Division) trained around the Yale Bowl / Yale campus, New Haven, Connecticut (CT Humanities, CT Explored, CT Military Dept).
- OWNER: J. Robert Conroy (James Robert Conroy, 1892–1987), a private and later a corporal. The NYT 1926 and CT Explored say Corporal, and CT Humanities says Private. Safest: "a young soldier named Robert Conroy". He named the dog Stubby for his short, stubby tail.
- PETS WERE BANNED in camp; the men adopted him anyway and he learned drills and bugle calls. Several accounts add that he learned a "salute" (raising his right paw).
- SMUGGLED TO FRANCE: October 1917, aboard the troop transport SS Minnesota (CT Military Dept, CT Humanities, CT Explored). NYT 1926: Conroy hid him in a greatcoat going up the gangway and then hid him in a coal bunker. Story: when an officer found him, Stubby saluted and was allowed to stay. This is an anecdote from period accounts, so introduce it with "the story goes".
- FIRST FRONT LINE: 5 Feb 1918, Chemin des Dames sector north of Soissons (CT Explored, Wikipedia; NYT: "more than a month" under fire at the Chemin des Dames).
- GAS: he was gassed, hospitalized, and recovered. Afterwards he was sensitive to gas and warned the men (NYT 1926 "wounded and gassed"; CT Military Dept "gassed a few times"; WWI Museum/Smithsonian resource).
- WOUNDED: April 1918, raid on Seicheprey (Lorraine). Grenade shrapnel hit his foreleg and/or chest (CT Military Dept, CT Humanities, Wikipedia). Treated at a hospital, where he went round the wounded men's beds (CT Humanities).
- FOUND WOUNDED MEN in no man's land and barked until help came (WWI Museum/Smithsonian resource; CT Explored hedges "reported to have"). Present as "credited with".
- CHATEAU-THIERRY: after the town was retaken in summer 1918, local women sewed him a chamois coat to carry his medals and stripes (CT Humanities, Wikipedia). The coat with the medals is the one in the Smithsonian.
- "17 BATTLES": a 1922 newspaper headline called him "Dog Hero of 17 Battles" (CT Military Dept). The WWI Museum/Smithsonian says 17 battles; the NYT says four major offensives. Say "four major offensives and seventeen engagements, by the count of his day".
- HOME: went home with the regiment in 1919 and led or marched in parades (CT Humanities, NYT).
- MEDAL: in 1921 General John J. Pershing presented him with a gold medal from the Humane Education Society (CT Military Dept; photo on Commons; Wikipedia).
- PRESIDENTS: the NYT says he was known to three presidents and met Wilson on Christmas Day at Mandres-en-Bassigny. CT Humanities mentions Harding and Coolidge.
- MEMBERSHIPS: American Legion; lifetime YMCA membership ("three bones a day") (CT Explored, Military Times, Wikipedia).
- GEORGETOWN: Conroy studied law at Georgetown and Stubby became the Hoyas' mascot, nudging a football around the field at halftime (CT Military Dept, CT Humanities, Wikipedia/The Hoya 2003).
- DEATH: 16 March 1926, in his sleep, of old age (Military Times: "died in Conroy's arms March 16, 1926"; Wikipedia: "died in his sleep"; CT Public 2026 "in Conroy's arms"). The NYT obituary ran 4 Apr 1926: "Stubby is dead." Safest wording: "In March 1926, Stubby died, with Conroy beside him."
- PRESERVED: his skin was mounted over a plaster cast, with his cremated remains sealed inside (CT Explored, Wikipedia, Atlas Obscura). Conroy gave him to the Smithsonian in 1956 (Wikipedia, Military Times). He is now in "The Price of Freedom: Americans at War", National Museum of American History, Washington DC (WWI Museum, CT Humanities). The NYT in 1926 already said: "His Arlington is to be the Smithsonian Institution." (exact quote)
- LOANS: on loan to the Connecticut National Guard (state armory exhibit) for some years, returned to the Smithsonian in 2003 (CT Military Dept).
- LEGACY: a brick in the Walk of Honor at the Liberty Memorial, Kansas City (2006; CT Humanities, Wikipedia, Commons photo). "Stubby Salutes", a bronze by Susan Bahary, at Veterans Memorial Park, Middletown CT, May 2018 (Wikipedia; Commons monument photos). A new statue at the Connecticut State Library, Hartford, 20 Apr 2026 (Army.mil, CT Public).
- Conroy never had another dog (Smithsonian Sidedoor/Bausum and CT Public 2026).

## Extra detail from the Smithsonian (Sidedoor transcript, read in full)
- Curator Jennifer Jones: Stubby is "a brindle terrier"; the mount is his brindled skin with his cremated remains inside. About 25 lb.
- Conroy was 25 years old at Yale. Stubby slept, ate and marched with him and learned the bugle and drill commands.
- Conroy taught him to "salute" (sitting up with a paw by his face). When officers found the smuggled dog in France, he saluted and they let him stay as mascot.
- Smuggling: a crewman helped hide him in the ship's coal bin until they were far out at sea. Getting off, Conroy wrapped him in his jacket and soldiers bunched around them.
- Conroy made him a gas mask. Stubby barked at gas, and the alarms went off.
- Wound: during a lull he ran into no man's land and a late shell or grenade burst. Conroy reportedly carried him to the medics. Six weeks in hospital, visiting other wounded men. (Matches Seicheprey, April 1918.)
- Bausum: when a wounded man might not survive, Stubby "would stay with them, and be their final companion" (her description).
- The spy: Bausum says it is unclear what the German was doing ("Maybe he was deserting... Maybe he was... spying"). Stubby grabbed him "probably by the seat of the pants". His Iron Cross became Stubby's (later lost, per CT Military Dept).
- Pershing pinned a medal on him. He met President Coolidge. Georgetown halftime.
- "when he died of old age in 1926, the nation mourned". Obituaries ran in the Washington Post and the NYT.
- An editorial of the day, quoted by the Smithsonian: "Stubby was the concentration of all we like in human beings, and lacked everything we dislike in them." (Use only as "one newspaper wrote...", with a Smithsonian credit; the original paper is not identified.)
- There was talk of Arlington, but Conroy chose the Smithsonian. CONROY NEVER HAD ANOTHER DOG; he married twice and lived to 95 (Bausum in Smithsonian podcast, plus CT Public 2026, so 2 sources). Bausum says Conroy "would say, 'Stubby got me through the war.'" This is reported speech via Bausum; attribute it that way and do not present it as a recorded quote.
- Donation: 22 May 1956 (NMAH story snippet; SI Archives photo dated 1956).

## Disputed points: safest wording
- RANK: no official record of a promotion. Bausum, the author of two books on Stubby, said "I'm quite convinced that he was promoted by the internet." (Military Times 2017, exact quote; credit Ann Bausum, Military Times). His three chevrons were overseas-service stripes, not sergeant's stripes. ON SCREEN: "The men called him 'Sergeant' Stubby. It was a nickname, not an official rank."
- SPY: the NYT 1926 says it happened at the Chemin des Dames; CT Military Dept says the Argonne. Both describe him holding a German by the seat of the pants or breeches until soldiers came. ON SCREEN: "According to newspaper accounts of his day, Stubby once caught a German who had slipped into the lines and held on to the seat of his pants until soldiers arrived." Give no location.
- PURPLE HEART: several web pages claim he got one or two. The modern Purple Heart was created on 22 Feb 1932 by War Department General Orders No. 3 (Army.mil "Five things you might not know about the Purple Heart": https://www.army.mil/article/249140), six years after Stubby died. He had a WOUND STRIPE on his coat. ON SCREEN: "a wound stripe on his coat".
- "Most decorated dog": an unsourced superlative. Say "one of the most celebrated dogs in American history".
- "First US war dog" or "first military dog": wrong. The US Army had no official dog program until 1942. Say "a mascot, long before the Army had any official war dogs".
- CONROY'S RANK: say "Robert Conroy" or "Private, later Corporal, Conroy".
- Statue reports call him "Connecticut's first military working dog". That is a modern label, so avoid it.

## Myths and popular errors to correct on screen
1. "Sergeant" was not an official rank; it was a nickname.
2. No Purple Heart. The medal did not exist in its modern form until 1932.
3. The spy is a period account with no official record, and its location varies.
4. Not "the first US military dog". He was an unofficial mascot.
5. The 2018 animated film "Sgt. Stubby: An American Hero" dramatizes; do not use its clips or stills (copyright, and fictional scenes).
6. Breed claims (Boston terrier or pit bull) are guesses. Say "a stray of unknown breed".

## Emotional peaks (true)
1. A stray wanders into a camp full of young soldiers at Yale and one of them, Robert Conroy, decides to keep him. Pets were banned.
2. Smuggling: wrapped in a greatcoat and hidden in the ship's coal bunker to cross the Atlantic, because Conroy wouldn't leave him behind.
3. Wounded by a German grenade at Seicheprey, he recovers and goes round the hospital beds of the wounded men.
4. The women of liberated Château-Thierry sew him a coat for his medals.
5. 1926: he dies of old age with Conroy beside him. Conroy keeps him, gives him to the nation in 1956, never owns another dog, and lives to 95.

## Hook ideas (true)
- "In 1917, a stray dog with a stub of a tail wandered into an army camp in Connecticut. Within a year he was in the trenches of France, and today he stands in the Smithsonian."
- "Pets were banned, so a young soldier hid a stray dog under his coat and smuggled him to war."
