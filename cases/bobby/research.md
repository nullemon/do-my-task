## CONFIRMED (documented, 2+ sources)
- **A small terrier called Bobby lived in and around Greyfriars Kirkyard**, in Edinburgh's Old Town, in the 1860s. He was fed by locals, especially **John Traill**, whose temperance coffee house was at Greyfriars Place. [Museum of Edinburgh; QMUL; Scottish Banner]
- **1867, the licence:** a new law (the Dog Duty / licensing rules of 1867) meant unlicensed dogs could be destroyed. Bobby had no legal owner. The **Lord Provost, Sir William Chambers**, paid his licence and gave him a **brass-plated collar inscribed "Greyfriars Bobby from the Lord Provost 1867 licensed"**. The collar is on display at the **Museum of Edinburgh**. [Museum of Edinburgh; EDCH; QMUL; Wikipedia] Chambers was also a director of the Scottish SPCA. [Wikipedia; single source, so it is optional]
- The **Traill family** gave Bobby's **dinner dish** to the Museum of Edinburgh. [Museum of Edinburgh]
- **Photographs:** Edinburgh publisher **Walter Greenoak Patterson** took several photos of Bobby around 1867–68, including Bobby with the Traill family. A c.1865 albumen print is in the National Galleries of Scotland (PGP R 831). [Scottish Banner; Commons/NGS metadata; Wikipedia]
- **Painting:** a portrait of Bobby by the animal painter **Gourlay Steell** was given to Traill. The engraving is in the Museum of Edinburgh. [Museum of Edinburgh]
- **Death: 14 January 1872.** The Scotsman printed an obituary (January 1872), and it was reprinted across Scotland and in London. [QMUL; Wikipedia] He was buried just inside the gate of Greyfriars Kirkyard, not far from John Gray's grave [Wikipedia]. That it was unconsecrated ground is often said but unverified.
- **Statue and fountain:** paid for by **Baroness Angela Burdett-Coutts**; sculptor **William Brodie**; bronze terrier on a red granite drinking fountain at the corner of George IV Bridge and Candlemaker Row. It originally had a lower basin for dogs. **Unveiled November 1873** (15 Nov 1873 per secondary sources); listed Category A in 1977. [HES LB27899; Museum of Edinburgh; EDCH; QMUL]
- **Headstones:** Bobby's red granite headstone was erected by the Dog Aid Society of Scotland and unveiled by the Duke of Gloucester on **13 May 1981**. It reads "Greyfriars Bobby – Died 14th January 1872 – Aged 16 years – Let his loyalty and devotion be a lesson to us all". [Wikipedia; Commons photos show the stone] A memorial stone to John Gray, "erected by American lovers of Bobby", stands in the kirkyard [1928 NZ newspaper via PapersPast; Commons photo]. Its year is not confirmed.
- **Disney film (1961):** *Greyfriars Bobby: The True Story of a Dog* (Walt Disney Productions), with Donald Crisp as James Brown, the kirkyard keeper, and Laurence Naismith as Traill. It was based on Eleanor Atkinson's **1912 novel**, not on records. A Skye terrier played Bobby. [Wikipedia; Britannica filmography of Donald Crisp]
- **Nose rubbing:** tourists rub the statue's nose "for luck". This is **a modern tourist habit, not Scottish folklore**. It has worn the patina off. The council repaired it in 2013 (cost £500) and asked people not to rub it; the repaired nose was deliberately scraped with an abrasive within days. Campaigns ("Hands off Bobby") followed. [Edinburgh Reporter 2013; Scotsman; BBC 2014 via Wikipedia]

## THE LEGEND (say "the story goes" / "according to the traditional story")
- Bobby belonged to **John Gray**, an Edinburgh City Police night watchman (some sources say a gardener who joined the police), who died of tuberculosis on **15 Feb 1858** and was buried in Greyfriars Kirkyard. [Wikipedia; EDCH; Museum of Edinburgh gives 1858]
- Bobby then refused to leave the grave for **14 years**, leaving each day at the **one o'clock gun** to eat at Traill's, and sleeping on the grave in all weathers. [EDCH; Wikipedia]
- James Brown, the kirkyard curator, tried to chase him out and gave in.

## DISPUTED: use the SAFEST WORDING
| Point | Conflict | Say this |
|---|---|---|
| The 14-year vigil | No independent record shows him at one grave for 14 years. 19th-century Europe had dozens of "cemetery dogs": strays fed by visitors and keepers until they lived there. Bondeson counts more than 60 (Wikipedia) or forty-six (QMUL). | "For years, Bobby lived in Greyfriars Kirkyard, and Edinburgh came to believe he was guarding his master's grave." |
| Who the owner was | Two John Grays: a police night watchman (buried at Greyfriars, 1858) and a farmer or shepherd (Traill's later account; reportedly buried elsewhere). Traill only bought the coffee house about four years after Gray died. | "His master is usually named as John Gray, a police night watchman. Even that is debated." |
| **Jan Bondeson's theory** (Cardiff University; book, 2011) | (1) Bobby was a stray, kept in the kirkyard with food from locals; (2) the story was a publicity "fabrication" by kirkyard curator **James Brown** and **John Traill**, good for business; (3) **two dogs**: the first, a scruffy mongrel, died around 1867 and was replaced by a younger dog that lived to 1872, because pictures change around 1867. His quotes, via Reuters 2011: "He was not a mourning dog at all — he was a happy little dog." / "A dead bobby was no good for business." | Present it as one historian's theory: "One historian, Jan Bondeson, believes..." Bondeson himself said the story can never be fully debunked. |
| Against the two-dog theory | Jakob Assarsson (2024) studied the photographs. He argues one dog appears in all genuine images, a terrier cross, and that the "second Bobby" image is a mislabelled or traced picture; a replacement of a different breed would have been noticed. QMUL's Liz Gray reports the theory without endorsing it. | "Others who have studied the photographs believe there was only ever one Bobby." |
| Breed | Skye terrier (the tradition, the Disney film); Dandie Dinmont (Mike Macbeth, 2022); "a terrier cross, resembling no breed in particular" (Assarsson 2024). The Museum of Edinburgh says "Skye terrier or possibly a Dandie Dinmont". | "A little terrier, traditionally called a Skye terrier." |
| Age 16 | Rests on the 1858 start date | "He died in January 1872." |
| Cause of death | Cancer of the jaw, from a necropsy by Prof Thomas Walley (Wikipedia, single source) | Don't use it, or say "he died, old and ill". |
| Statue date | Some sources say Brodie made it in 1872; it was unveiled in Nov 1873 | "A year after he died, a statue..." |
| Licence paid by Chambers | Widely reported. Some versions involve a court summons for Traill, which may come from the film. | Say only: the Lord Provost paid it and gave him the collar (the collar exists). |

## MYTHS AND POPULAR ERRORS TO CORRECT ON SCREEN
- "Auld Jock the shepherd": this comes from Eleanor Atkinson's 1912 novel and the Disney film, not from records.
- "Bobby is buried with his master": no. He is buried near the gate, apart from Gray.
- "Rubbing his nose is an old Scottish tradition for luck": no. It's a recent tourist habit that damages the statue.
- The 2005/06 film used a West Highland terrier, and the Disney film used a purebred Skye. Neither looks like the real Bobby photos.
- "Disney's 'True Story'": the subtitle is marketing; the film follows the novel.
- Watch for "Greyfriar's Bobby" with an apostrophe; the correct form is "Greyfriars".

## EMOTIONAL PEAK MOMENTS (true or properly attributed)
1. 1867: a dog with no owner faces destruction under the new law, and the Lord Provost of Edinburgh himself pays for his licence. The brass collar still exists. (Documented.)
2. A whole neighbourhood feeds a little dog: Traill's coffee house keeps his dish, and that dish is now in a museum. (Documented.)
3. January 1872: Bobby dies, and The Scotsman prints an obituary for a dog, reprinted as far away as London. (Documented.)
4. A wealthy stranger, Baroness Burdett-Coutts, pays for a statue and a drinking fountain with a basin for thirsty dogs. (Documented.)
5. 1981: a headstone reading "Let his loyalty and devotion be a lesson to us all." (Documented.)
6. The twist: was it loyalty, or a beloved stray and a good story? Let viewers decide. (Comment bait.)
